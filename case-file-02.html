Field Report 02

The Fix Was Slower, Not Faster

Years ago I took over a production line running an 80% rejection rate. Everyone, myself included at first, assumed the defect was happening at the critical processing step. Turned out that wasn't where the problem actually was.

The starting problem

The defect appeared only during Non-Destructive Testing, the final inspection gate before a part left the line. By that point, every rejected part had consumed a full cycle of manpower, machine time, and processing capacity. The late detection made the 80% rejection rate particularly costly.

Earlier investigations had focused on the critical processing step because that was where the defect became visible. Repeated parameter adjustments had not improved the rejection rate. I therefore examined the conditions before and after the operation rather than continuing to tune the same step.

What I found

The root cause was the thermal profile surrounding the operation: how the component was heated and cooled before and after the critical step. Once I traced the defect to those conditions, the rejection pattern could be explained and addressed.

The hard sell

Here's what made it a hard sell: fixing it meant adding a step to the process, which meant cycle time went up. Nobody on a floor already fighting to hit takt time wants to hear "we're making this slower." Making the case meant walking people past the per-part cycle time and showing them what the 80% rejection rate was actually costing.

The cost of rejection included more than the original processing time. It also included rework, replacement production, scrap, and the capacity consumed by components that would ultimately be rejected. On that basis, one additional step was less costly than continuing with the existing rejection rate.

Where it landed

Rejection dropped from 80% to 15%, while output increased to 110 units per shift despite the additional process step. Reducing scrap and rework released capacity that had previously been consumed by rejected parts.

What I still use from this

The experience reinforced the need to separate the point where a defect becomes visible from the process condition that causes it. It also showed that a longer individual cycle can improve total output when it prevents scrap and releases wasted capacity.
