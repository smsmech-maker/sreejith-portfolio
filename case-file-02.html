Field Report 02

The Fix Was Slower, Not Faster

Years ago I took over a production line running an 80% rejection rate. Everyone, myself included at first, assumed the defect was happening at the critical processing step. Turned out that wasn't where the problem actually was.

The starting problem

The defect only ever showed up at Non-Destructive Testing, the final inspection gate right before a part left the line. Which meant every rejected part had already eaten a full cycle of manpower, machine time, and processing capacity before anyone caught it. An 80% rejection rate at that stage is a different kind of expensive than an 80% rejection rate would be if it happened early — you're not failing cheap, you're failing after the line has already paid for the part in full and has nothing to show for it.

By the time I got there, everyone on the floor already had a theory, and it centered on the critical processing step — that's where the defect physically showed up, so that's where people kept adjusting parameters. Weeks of tuning hadn't moved the rejection rate at all. Turned out the step was just where the defect became visible, not where it originated, and nobody had gone looking further back.

What I found

The actual issue turned out to be thermal — how the part was heating and cooling around that step. The fix lived in the temperature profile before and after it, nowhere near the parameter everyone kept adjusting. Once I traced it there, the pattern in the rejects finally made sense.

The hard sell

Here's what made it a hard sell: fixing it meant adding a step to the process, which meant cycle time went up. Nobody on a floor already fighting to hit takt time wants to hear "we're making this slower." Making the case meant walking people past the per-part cycle time and showing them what the 80% rejection rate was actually costing.

People price a rejected part by the time it took to make. That's not the real number — you have to add the rework, the scrap replacement, and the capacity it quietly used up on its way to getting thrown out. Once you count all of that, one extra step per part was cheaper than the 80% we were losing.

Where it landed

Rejection dropped from 80% to 15%, and output still climbed to 110 units a shift — on a line serving a demanding tier-one client base, and despite the extra step. The line simply wasn't burning capacity anymore on parts that were headed for scrap regardless.

What I still use from this

When a process is under pressure, the instinct is always to strip steps out to save time. Sometimes that's backwards — one more step, placed in the right spot, is the actual fix. What this taught me is to keep "where the defect appears" and "where the defect is caused" as two separate questions, because they're rarely the same place, and chasing the first one just burns time tuning a parameter that was never really the problem.
