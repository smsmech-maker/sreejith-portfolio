Field Report 01

Building a PMO From Zero

In January 2022, I inherited a department, not a PMO. I had just joined a Tier-1 automotive components manufacturer, and its Switch-systems division had NPD projects running — sixty-plus of them, at any given time — but no shared definition of what "on track" meant, no gate criteria two people would agree on, and no single place to see the whole portfolio at once. My job title said Deputy Manager. The actual job was: build the structure this department has been missing.

The starting problem

Sixty-plus concurrent projects sounds like a portfolio. In practice it was sixty-plus separate conversations, each with its own informal sense of progress. Engineering thought a project was "basically done" when the design was frozen. Quality thought the same project was "at risk" because validation hadn't started. Supply chain didn't know it existed yet. Nobody was wrong — there was just no shared gate structure to be right or wrong against.

What I built, in order

I didn't start with software. I started with criteria: what does a project have to demonstrate to move from concept to feasibility, from feasibility to design freeze, from design freeze to validation, from validation to serial production. I used APQP as the skeleton and QCDDM — Quality, Cost, Delivery, Development, Management — as the scorecard at each gate, because both were already partially familiar to the teams; the goal was structure, not a new vocabulary to fight over.

Then I built the gate-passage review itself: a recurring forum where a project could only advance with evidence against the criteria, not a status update. The first few reviews were uncomfortable — projects that everyone had assumed were healthy didn't clear the bar. That discomfort was the point. It meant the criteria were doing something real instead of rubber-stamping.

Only after the criteria and the review cadence were working did I standardize the tooling — introducing Zoho Projects as the department's shared system of record, so the gate status of any project was visible in one place instead of living in someone's inbox.

The hard part was never the spreadsheet. It was getting engineering, quality, and supply chain to accept the same definition of "ready."

Where it landed

Within the cycle, the portfolio sustained a success rate above 85%, with roughly a dozen projects transitioning into serial production against plan. Cost discipline enforced at each gate — rather than caught late — contributed to a high-single-digit margin improvement over the following year. First Time Right performance, a proxy for how much rework the new structure was preventing, moved from the low 60s to over 80%.

What I'd tell someone starting the same job

Don't buy a tool first. A shared, contested, eventually-agreed definition of what "ready to advance" means is the actual PMO. The software is just where you make that definition visible. If you skip straight to the dashboard, you get a very well-organized version of the same disagreement you started with.
