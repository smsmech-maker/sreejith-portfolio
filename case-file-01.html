Field Report 01

Building a PMO From Zero

I joined a Tier-1 automotive components manufacturer in January 2022 as Deputy Manager for the Switch-systems division. On paper, that meant overseeing NPD projects — sixty-plus running at any given time. What I actually walked into was a department with no shared idea of what "on track" meant, no gate criteria two people would agree on, and no single place to see the whole portfolio at once. So that became the real job: building the structure that was missing.

The starting problem

Calling it a portfolio was generous. What we had was sixty-plus separate, informal conversations about progress, and everyone had their own version of it. Engineering considered a project basically done once the design was frozen. Quality considered the same project at risk because validation hadn't even started. Supply chain, more often than not, hadn't heard of it yet. Nobody was being difficult on purpose — there just wasn't anything shared to measure against.

What I built, in order

Software wasn't the first move — criteria were. I worked out what a project actually had to show to move from concept to feasibility, feasibility to design freeze, design freeze to validation, and validation to serial production. For the framework I leaned on APQP, and used QCDDM (Quality, Cost, Delivery, Development, Management) as the scorecard at each gate, mostly because both were already somewhat familiar to the teams — the goal was to build structure, not hand people a new vocabulary to argue about on top of everything else.

Then came the gate-passage review: a recurring session where a project could only move forward with actual evidence, not a status update claiming things were fine. The first few reviews were rough — projects everyone had assumed were healthy didn't clear the bar, and there was some grumbling about it. I took that as a decent sign. It meant the criteria were doing something real instead of rubber-stamping whatever people already believed.

The tooling came last, once the criteria and the review rhythm were actually working. I brought in Zoho Projects as the shared system of record, so gate status for any project lived in one place instead of scattered across people's inboxes.

Getting a spreadsheet built is the easy part. Getting engineering, quality, and supply chain to agree on what "ready" actually means took most of the year.

Where it landed

Over that cycle the portfolio held a success rate above 85%, and around a dozen projects made it into serial production on plan. Catching cost issues at the gate instead of downstream also helped drive a high-single-digit margin improvement the following year. First Time Right — a decent proxy for how much rework the structure was preventing — moved from the low 60s to over 80%.

What I'd tell someone starting the same job

If I were doing this again, I'd still hold off on buying a tool first. The PMO isn't the software — it's the argued-over, eventually-agreed definition of what "ready to advance" means. The dashboard is just where that definition becomes visible once you actually have one. Skip straight to the dashboard and what you've really built is a tidy, well-organized record of the same disagreement you started with.
